The story in 60 seconds

We offered PA and Life insurance to about 214,000 customers and 1.2% bought. That's low, but it isn't random. Young customers, active accounts with money flowing in, and customers who opened their account in the last few years buy two to three times more often than dormant ones. Affluent customers prefer Life; the mass market prefers PA. I turned this into a model that ranks customers: calling only the top 20% would reach about half of all buyers. So my recommendation is to score customers before each campaign, offer each one the right product, and stop mass-contacting people who almost never buy.

The one definition to know: acceptance rate = customers who bought ÷ customers contacted in that group. The overall average is 1.22%.

Is 1.2% bad?
For outbound insurance cross-sell, low single-digit response is normal. The point is not the level; it's that the response is very uneven across customers, which is what makes targeting valuable.
Slide 3

Data & approach

~1.5 min

Purpose: show you understand the data and handled its problems. Panels for data roles always check this.

What to explain
  • The file: 215,993 rows, 1 row = 1 customer who received an offer; 26 columns (demographics, segment, product holding, balances, cash flow, months on book) plus the outcome label (0 = reject, 1 = PA, 2 = Life).
  • Analysed 214,493: 1,500 rows had every customer field empty, and only 0.07% of them bought (1 in 1,500), so they look like unmatched records and were removed.
  • Pipeline: load → quality check → clean → analyse → model. It's written as Python classes and fully reproducible with python main.py.
  • Main data issues:
    • The 15-day inflow, outflow and net-flow fields are identical to the 30-day fields in 100% of rows, so they're copies and were dropped.
    • currentacc_bal is always 0, so it was dropped.
    • Marital status is in Thai with overlapping values; it was translated and merged.
    • Only 1.2% of rows are positive, which is why accuracy is useless here.
The data is one campaign file: about 216,000 offers with 26 customer fields and the outcome, reject, PA or Life. Before analysing it I checked the quality and found nine issues. The important ones: 1,500 rows were completely empty, the 15-day cash-flow columns are exact copies of the 30-day ones, and current-account balance is always zero. I removed or fixed those, and everything runs as a reproducible Python pipeline.
Why not remove the 2,173 duplicate rows?
There's no customer ID, so I can't prove they're the same person. Two different customers can have identical values. I kept them and flagged it as a limitation.
Couldn't the empty rows be real customers?
Possibly, but with no attributes they can't be analysed or targeted, and their 0.07% response says they aren't comparable. Including them would only dilute the rates.

How many customers were contacted each month (left), and what share of them bought (right).

Key numbers
  • Jun–Sep: about 10–11k customers a month, acceptance 1.75–1.95% (peak Aug 1.95%).
  • Big months (Jan–Apr, Oct–Dec): about 22–24k a month, acceptance 0.82–1.30% (lowest Feb 0.82%).

On the left is how many customers we contacted each month; on the right, how many of them bought. When volume halves in June to September, the acceptance rate nearly doubles, to about 1.9%. So more contacts didn't bring proportionally more sales. Notice the swing is almost all PA (the blue line); Life stays flat.
Careful: don't claim volume causes the lower rate. The smaller campaigns may have targeted different customers or used a different offer, and there's no year to separate seasonality. Say "is associated with".
Could it just be seasonality?
It could be. Without a year in the data I can't separate season from campaign design. That's why I'd test it: run a tighter, model-targeted list in a big month and compare.

This chart combines size and quality. Each bubble is an age group within a segment: further right means more customers, higher means a better rate, and a bigger bubble means more buyers. Slide 11

Propensity model: half the buyers with a fifth of the calls

~1.5 min
Cumulative gains curve
Permutation importance
What the model is

A gradient-boosted decision-tree model (scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingClassifier) that gives every customer a score: the probability of accepting any offer. It was trained on 70% of the customers (150,145) and evaluated on the other 30% (64,348) that it never saw.

How to read the gains curve (left)
  • Sort customers from highest to lowest score. x = % of customers contacted; y = % of all buyers reached.
  • The straight diagonal is random calling (call 20%, get 20% of buyers). The curve is the model. The bigger the gap, the better.
  • The marked point: top 20% → 49% of buyers. Also top 10% → 30%, top 30% → 62%, top 50% → 82%.
How to read importance (right)
  • Each feature is shuffled randomly; the bar is how much the model's ROC-AUC drops. A bigger drop means the model relies on it more.
  • Inflow 30d (0.100) and campaign month (0.069) lead, then months on book (0.028) and age (0.021). This matches slides 4–8.
The three tiles
  • ROC-AUC 0.72: the chance the model ranks a random buyer above a random non-buyer (0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect).
  • Top 20% reach 49%, and top-10% lift 3.0×: the top decile buys at 3.6% against the 1.2% average.
Next I built a model that scores every customer. I tested it on 64,000 customers it had never seen. If we sort them by score and call only the top 20%, we reach 49% of all buyers, against 20% if we called at random. What the model relies on matches the analysis: cash inflow, the campaign, tenure and age.
Is 0.72 AUC good enough?
For campaign response with mostly static customer data, 0.70–0.80 is a typical, useful range. What matters commercially is the lift: 3× in the top decile and 49% of buyers in 20% of the list.
Isn't campaign month a form of leakage?
Not leakage, because we know which campaign a customer is in before calling. But it captures campaign-level effects rather than the customer. For a pure customer score I'd drop it and re-check the AUC; inflow, tenure and age still carry most of the signal. That's a planned next step.
Did you handle the class imbalance?
The goal is ranking, not a yes/no label, so I evaluated with ranking metrics (ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, gains) rather than accuracy, and used a stratified split so both sets keep the 1.2% rate. Resampling isn't needed for ranking.

How to read the decile chart
  • The test customers are sorted by score and cut into 10 equal slices: D1 = top 10%, D10 = bottom 10%.
  • Bar height = lift = slice acceptance ÷ average. The line at 1× is the average.
  • A falling staircase means the model ranks well: D1 3.0×, D2 1.9×, D3 1.3×, D4 1.15×, then below average from D5 (0.85×) down to D10 (0.13×).

TierWhoAcceptanceShare of buyersTreatment
ATop 20%2.99%49%Agent call, best offer
BNext 30%1.34%33%Standard campaign
CBottom 50%≈0.44%18%Digital only or skip
To make the score usable, I cut the list into ten slices. The top slice buys at three times the average, and the bottom five slices are all below average. So I propose three tiers: Tier A, the top 20%, gets an agent call and has half of the buyers; Tier B gets the standard campaign; and Tier C, half of the list but only 18% of the buyers, moves to cheap digital channels or is skipped.
How much money would this save?
I don't have cost-per-call or premium values. With them, the cut-off becomes a simple calculation: keep calling while the expected premium of the next decile is above the cost of calling it.

Recommendations

~1 min
  1. Score before every campaign: call Tier A first. The top 30% reaches 62% of buyers.
  2. Match the product: Life to Upper Mass, income ≥30k and credit-card holders; PA to the active mass market.
  3. Prioritise active and young customers: inflow >5k, 1–36 months on book, age ≤30, savings 1k–20k (1.5–2.1% vs the 1.2% average).
  4. Fewer, better contacts in the big months; Jun–Sep reached about twice the rate.
  5. Fix the data: 15-day flows, tenure, and add a customer ID plus the Policy and Payment data.
Five actions. First, score customers before every campaign. Second, offer the right product: Life to the affluent, PA to the active mass market. Third, prioritise young and active customers. Fourth, contact fewer, better customers in the big months. And fifth, fix the data issues so the next model is even better.
Slide 14

Limitations & next steps

~45 s

Purpose: show judgement. Naming limits before the panel does builds trust.

  • No customer ID: duplicates can't be confirmed, and data can't be joined to other sources.
  • No year: seasonality can't be separated from trend.
  • Tenure quality: 26% are 0 (likely unknown), and 839 are impossible (longer than adult life).
  • No Policy or Payment data: we optimise for the first sale, not for customers who keep paying.
  • Correlation, not causation: the model ranks; it doesn't prove why people buy. That's why we A/B test.
Next steps

An A/B test of model targeting, a PA-vs-Life product-choice model, validation on a later time period, joining Policy and Payment data, and a scheduled monthly run.

What would you do with one more week?
An uplift model, to find customers who buy because we call rather than those who'd buy anyway, plus a time-based validation and a cost/benefit cut-off with real premium and call costs.

Method details (for technical questions)

Data preparation

  • Removed 1,500 rows with every customer field empty (0.07% acceptance).
  • Dropped inflow1_15, outflow1_15 and net_flow_15d (identical to the 30-day fields), plus currentacc_bal and avg_currentaccbal_30d (always or almost always 0).
  • Translated marital status (Thai → English) and merged the overlapping "Other" values; occupations under 500 customers were folded into "Other".
  • Bands: age (≤25 … 56+), income (≤5k … 50k+), tenure (0, 1–12, 13–36, 37–60, 61–120, 120+), savings, inflow.
  • Heatmap and bubble cells need ≥300 customers; the index chart needs ≥1,000.

Model

  • Target: accepted any offer (PA or Life) vs rejected.
  • 20 features: 8 categorical (campaign month, marital status, occupation, segment, gender, travel card, credit card, payroll) and 12 numeric (children, age, income, debit-card balance and spend, payments, savings balance and average, months on book, inflow, outflow, net flow).
  • HistGradientBoostingClassifier: native categorical handling, learning rate 0.05, up to 400 trees with early stopping (15% validation), 31 leaves, L2 = 1.0. Missing values are handled natively.
  • Stratified 70/30 split (random_state = 42). Metrics: ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, cumulative gains, decile lift.
  • Permutation importance on 20,000 test customers, 3 repeats, scored by ROC-AUC.

Code

An OOP Python package (src/campaign_analysis): CampaignDataLoader → DataQualityChecker → DataCleaner → FeatureEngineer → ResponseAnalyzer + PropensityModel → HtmlBuilder → PdfExporter, orchestrated by CampaignAnalysisPipeline. It has 12 pytest unit tests. The dashboard, this deck and this guide are all generated from the same run, so the numbers always match.

Tough questions bank

Why not just report accuracy?
Predicting "no" for everyone is 98.8% accurate and completely useless. Ranking metrics show whether the model finds buyers.
Why did you use medians, not means?
Money fields are extremely skewed: savings has a mean of 20.9k but a median of 0.8k. A few very rich customers would dominate the mean.
Are the differences just noise?
For the main findings, no: the groups have tens of thousands of customers, so margins are about ±0.2 points. Small groups (e.g. the 726-customer heatmap cell) are noisier, so I quote patterns, not single cells.
Why does every segment have the same total rate?
The segments differ in what they buy, not how much. Upper Mass trades PA for Life. That's why product matching matters more than segment targeting.
Does the model overfit?
All the metrics come from a 30% hold-out the model never saw, with early stopping during training. The next check is validation on a later time period.
How would you put this into production?
The same pipeline runs monthly: score the customer base, write the tiers to the CRM, A/B test against current targeting, and monitor data quality and score drift.
Why one model for both products?
First decide whether to contact; then a second, product-choice model decides what to offer. The descriptive analysis already gives the product rules (slide 9); a dedicated model is the next step.
What about the Policy and Payment data mentioned in the brief?
It wasn't provided. With it, I'd add persistency (does the customer keep paying?) so we optimise for valuable customers, not just first sales.
Is the month effect causal?
Unknown. Smaller campaigns may have been targeted differently, and there's no year. I'd test it rather than assume it.
Why are Government employees so low?
They often already have employer or government insurance cover, so they need less from a bank offer. It's a plausible explanation, worth confirming with Policy data.
What is the biggest risk in your recommendation?
Selection bias: past campaigns already chose who to contact, so the model learns from that selection. An A/B test with a random control group protects against it.
